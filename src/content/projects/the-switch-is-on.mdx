<Chapter eyebrow="01 · The problem" title="Good tools nobody could reach.">
  <Media slot="media" src="sio/old-homepage-2024.webp" alt="The 2024 Switch Is On homepage: a beach hero photo, then tool cards and appliance sections further down" fit="cover" focus="top" ratio="16/10" large />

About 40% of visitors came for rebates, but the Incentive Finder sat below the fold, behind dense copy and stock photos. And the site looked so much like a lead marketplace that visitors kept asking what it was selling.

<Expander summary="What we found">

<Point title="Most visitors were on a phone" lead="52% of visitors use mobile, and iOS alone is 42%.">

The Incentive Finder already sat below the fold on desktop. On a phone it was even further down.

</Point>

<Point title="Few searches ended in a conversation" lead="Only 7.7% of people who searched for a contractor went on to contact one." />

<Point title="The nonprofit advantage was invisible" lead="SIO looked like Angi or Thumbtack.">

When the site tried to explain that it sold nothing, it made things worse: in testing, the more the site said what it wasn't, the more suspicious people became.

</Point>

<Point title="Every new program meant rebuilding" lead="Three finders had each grown their own layout, filters, cards and icons.">

Any new partner or brand meant redoing decisions that had already been made somewhere else.

</Point>

</Expander>
</Chapter>

<Chapter eyebrow="02 · The front door" title="One word, two paths.">
  <Media slot="media" src="sio/homepage-top.webp" alt="The Switch Is On homepage: the headline Modern electric appliances make happier, healthier homes, two choices below it, Take me to incentives and Find me a pro, and a row of photo and benefit cards" fit="cover" large outline />

"Switch" sounds like effort, "electrification" sounds technical, and "decarbonize" sounds political. We replaced all three with one benefit-led idea, *better*, and a homepage that asks one question: *Take me to incentives* or *Find me a pro*.

<Expander summary="How the homepage works" rows>

<Point title="Stop asking people to give something up" lead="Modern electric appliances are better for your health, your comfort, your wallet and your future.">

Visitors finish the sentence with whatever motivates them, and they don't get a lecture.

</Point>

<Cycle
  frames={['sio/better-cheaper.webp', 'sio/better-professional.webp', 'sio/better-efficient.webp', 'sio/better-easy.webp']}
  alt="Homepage section titled Better heating and cooling starts here, with four cards: Make it cheaper, Make it professional, Make it efficient and Make it easy, each taking a turn in its own color"
  ratio="200/129"
  size="half"
/>

<Point title="Five modules, one job each" lead="Below the two paths, each module can be measured and changed on its own.">

Contractors got their own entry point in the navigation and a Pros page in the same voice as the rest of the site.

</Point>

<Media src="sio/homepage-mobile-2up.webp" alt="Two phone screens from the site: a heat pump article, and the contractor sign-up banner above How can I go electric" fit="cover" ratio="5/4" focus="top" size="half" />

<Point title="Show trust instead of claiming it" lead="We cut the defensive “we don't sell anything” copy.">

Trust now comes through how the site behaves: methodology notes on tool pages, a quiet strip of partner logos, and data sources linked at the bottom of each page.

</Point>

<Chapter eyebrow="03 · Where trust gets decided" title="Trust is won on the card, not the profile.">
  <Video

Viewing a contractor's full profile only moves contact from 4.8% to 6.8%. Homeowners decide at the card in the results list, so that's where I put the proof, and where every partner wanted their badge.

<Expander summary="Proof, badges and the other side of the lead" rows>

<Point title="Use proof, not adjectives" lead="I wrote a framework that separates four kinds of signal that had been mixed together: contractor type, performance tier, program membership and manufacturer certification.">

The tier system is anchored to verified heat pump installs rather than lead volume. The first piece to ship was a "Verified Installs" counter, added to the existing card so it didn't have to wait for the full overhaul.

</Point>

<Media src="sio/card-anatomy.webp" alt="Contractor card guidelines: a card with labeled rows for logo, title, reviews, location, details and action, each with its height and padding" tone="sio" size="half" />

<Point title="Close the loop on the contractor side" lead="A lead only matters if a contractor answers it.">

I designed the lead flows in the [Contractor Hub](/work/contractor-hub): receive, acknowledge, accept or decline. In the 60 days after launch, leads acknowledged by a contractor went from 18% to 61%, and 824 were acknowledged in the first month alone. Response speed now counts toward a contractor's ranking in search.

</Point>

<Media src="sio/hub-leads.webp" alt="Contractor Hub leads page: new leads this week, a 90-day breakdown, the overall response rate marked Slow with a Why does this matter link, and lead cards with Decline and Accept buttons" fit="cover" outline size="half" />

<Point title="Every badge is a political decision" lead="Every partner program cared about whose badge showed and how prominently.">

My constraint was that badges never shrink. They got their own row above a pinned "Get in touch" button, and the results went from three columns to two so badges wouldn't wrap. I also raised an equity problem: ranking paid or partner badges first quietly pushes down the programs that don't pay.

</Point>

<Chapter eyebrow="04 · The system underneath" title="The system had stalled twice. I changed who decides.">
  <MediaGrid slot="media" cols={2}>

Two design-system efforts had stalled before mine, with pull requests closed without merging. Nobody owned the yes. I became the single owner for review, pages moved over one at a time as they were needed, and a 12-point checklist became the definition of done.

<Expander summary="Inside the 13 role colors">

<Point title="My sync tool wasn't the answer" lead="Colors lived in two places that never matched: 160 colors and 74 aliases in Figma, and separate theme files for each brand in code, kept in sync by hand.">

I built a `tokens.json` file and a custom Figma plugin to sync them automatically. Engineering didn't want an extra layer between Figma and code, so the plugin went unused.

</Point>

<Point title="13 role colors, named the same in every brand" lead="Instead of defending the pipeline, I simplified the system.">

The code theme files became the source of truth. With matching names in Figma and code, AI can compare them directly, and there's nothing left to map between the two.

</Point>

<Media src="sio/role-colors.webp" alt="Table of the 13 role colors with their values for SIO, Colorado Contractor Hub, Power Ahead Colorado and WattSipper" tone="hub" />

<Point title="New brands now ship faster" lead="WattSipper, BDC's first white-label, launched as an Incentive Finder set up as a configuration rather than built from scratch.">

When the team redesigned the Incentive Finder's detail view in April 2026, they built it quickly on this system. The share of searchers who opened an incentive's details went from 18% to 44%, and on iOS from 16% to 47%. Detail views went from 4,257 to 5,692 a month.

[See WattSipper's Incentive Finder](https://incentives.wattsipper.com/incentive-finder)

</Point>

</Expander>
